Passage

A travel app for small groups. It reads your bookings, tells you when to leave, and settles who owes whom.

The problem

A group trip lives in thirty emails, a spreadsheet and a chat.

Before the trip, bookings are scattered across inboxes. During it, nobody is sure what’s next, whether there’s time for coffee, or which train they need. After it, someone spends an evening working out who owes whom. Existing apps solve one of these at a time, and every one of them asks the whole group to sign up first.

Who it’s for

Two to six friends or family travelling across several cities, and especially the one person in every group who ends up holding it all together.

What it does
  • Reads your bookings. Screenshot a flight, hotel or train confirmation and the details fill themselves in. You check and save.
  • Keeps you on time. Next Up counts down to the next booking, works out when to leave, and warns you as you start running late.
  • Researches each city for you. Tap a city and Passage searches the web for places that fit each traveler’s interests, with prices where it found a real listing.
  • Settles up. Paid bookings land in the Ledger automatically, receipts can be scanned, and debts collapse into the fewest payments.
Decisions that shaped it
  1. No sign-up. A trip is a private link.

    WhyA group adopts a travel app in its first minute or not at all. Asking five people to create accounts loses most of them. The trade-off: whoever has the link has the trip, and a new phone means opening it from the link again. Sign-in is first on the roadmap.

  2. The AI proposes; the traveler confirms.

    WhyOne wrong date in a travel app costs a missed train and all future trust. Nothing read from a screenshot is saved silently, fields the model was unsure of are flagged, and uploading the same booking twice is caught.

  3. Show a booking link only when it’s real.

    WhyKlook is the one booking partner, and its links appear only where the research found an actual listing. The model is never allowed to construct a link or a price. Credibility is worth more than an affiliate click.

  4. The demo is always mid-trip.

    WhyThe live features only make sense during a trip. The sample trip rebuilds itself around today, so anyone opening the link sees Day 2 in Amsterdam, with a booking about an hour away that suits the time of day.

  5. Slow AI work must survive real life.

    WhyCity research takes about a minute, and people lock their phones. The server saves the results the moment they’re ready, so they’re waiting when the app is reopened.

How I built it

I worked as the product lead, with Claude writing the code. I set the scope, wrote the decisions down, and reviewed every change on my own phone before it could go live.

Change on its own branch→Tests + full screen check→My review link→Notes left in the app→My explicit yes→Live

Review notes are captured inside the app itself, tagged with the screen they were left on, so feedback turns into fixes without screenshots and long messages.

Built with

Next.js and TypeScript, installable on a phone like an app. Claude reads the bookings and researches each city with live web search. Supabase stores and syncs shared trips in real time, and Vercel hosts it.

What I left out

Accounts and sign-in, and booking inside the app: Passage links out to Klook instead. Both were cut to get a group from link to live trip in under a minute.

Next steps

From splitting what you spent to saving for the next trip.

  1. Sign-in.

    A trip follows you across devices, and each traveler has one verified identity. Everything below depends on it.

  2. A Ledger connected to your bank.

    With your consent, through India’s Account Aggregator network and a licensed partner, card and account spends made during the trip’s dates arrive in the Ledger on their own. Each one waits for the trip admin to approve it before it counts, so nothing is split by mistake. Card numbers are never stored; consent is given through the bank and can be withdrawn at any time.

  3. Save while spending.

    Savings rules inside the Ledger, in the spirit of Qapital, aimed at the group’s next trip:

    • Round-ups. Every trip spend is rounded up and the difference goes to the next trip’s fund.
    • Shared goals. “Lisbon 2027, ₹2 lakh”, with each traveler’s share and a progress bar on the boarding pass.
    • Rules you set. “Every taxi, put ₹100 aside”, or “when we come in under the day’s budget, save the difference”.
    • Settle up into savings. Pay a debt straight into the shared fund instead of to one person.

    Moving money needs a regulated banking partner, so the first version tracks goals and nudges each traveler to set money aside. Automatic transfers come with the partner.

  4. A concierge layer.

    For travelers who want the planning done for them: Passage drafts the trip, a person checks it, and bookings land in the itinerary already confirmed.

Thesis

A presales pitch engine. Give it a company and a seller; it researches, tests six hypotheses, keeps the two that hold, and writes a six-slide executive pitch where every number has a source.

The problem

A good presales pitch takes days of research. Most are written in an hour, from a template.

The pitches that win meetings start from the buyer’s own numbers: their filings, their results, the news about them. They name a specific problem, put a price on it, and ask for something small. That research takes days, so most pitches skip it and lead with the product. I wrote one the long way, for Lyzr on CVS Health compensation, and wanted to know whether the method itself could be made repeatable.

Who it’s for

Presales consultants, founders and strategy teams who have to walk into an executive’s office with a point of view on that company, not a generic deck.

What it does
  • Researches the target. Filings, latest results, recent news and the named executive who owns the problem, each fact kept with the page it came from.
  • Tests its own ideas. It proposes six hypotheses about where the buyer is losing people or money, looks for evidence for and against each, drops four with reasons, and keeps two that form a chain: a mechanism, then its price.
  • Does the arithmetic in code. Every calculation is re-run by the app, not trusted to the model, and shown in the footnote.
  • Writes in my format. Six slides: the situation in four figures, two hypotheses with a way to test them, a named agent as the solution and a 90-day pilot as the ask. Tap any footnote to see its source. Download as PowerPoint.
  • Takes your own material. Add PDFs or screenshots of charts; their figures are cited as material you provided.
Brief→Your files→Scout→Hypothesize→Test→Size→Write→Check
Decisions that shaped it
  1. No number without a source.

    WhyOne invented figure in front of a CFO ends the conversation. A fact is only kept if it cites a page the model actually opened, every slide number is traced back to a fact or a calculation, and anything that can’t be traced is flagged before the pitch is shown.

  2. Show the working, including what was thrown away.

    WhyThe hypotheses it dropped, and why, are what a reviewer trusts. Every pitch carries a research trail of searches, sources, the six candidates and the calculations.

  3. The cheaper model reads; the stronger one judges and writes.

    WhyMost of the cost is reading search results, so that runs on Sonnet. Choosing the hypotheses and writing the slides, the two steps a buyer judges, run on Opus. A full run costs about a dollar and a half.

  4. No database.

    WhyThe samples ship inside the app and my own runs stay on my device, so the app costs nothing to keep running and could be handed to someone else as is. The phone runs the eight steps one call at a time, which also keeps each step inside the server’s five-minute limit.

  5. Live runs behind a passcode; samples open to anyone.

    WhyEach run costs real money. Visitors see finished pitches instantly; only I can start a new one, and a run stops itself at $4.

How I built it

Same studio as Passage: I set the scope and the voice rules from my own CVS pitch, Claude wrote the code, and every change went to my review link before it could go live. The first real run, on Uber, surfaced two failures that tests with simulated answers could not: the model’s choice of hypotheses was being rejected over formatting, and the API refused a slide template as too large. Both were fixed on the review link the same afternoon, with tests added for each.

Built with

Next.js and TypeScript on Vercel. Claude does the research with live web search and page fetching, reads uploaded PDFs and chart images, and checks its own draft; a small calculator in the app re-runs every formula. The PowerPoint is built in the browser.

What I left out

The companion deck that maps the seller’s products and prices onto the problem, accounts for other users, and editing slides inside the app. You edit the downloaded PowerPoint instead.

Next steps
  1. More samples, made by the engine.

    A re-run of the CVS pitch to compare against the hand-made one, a Sarvam pitch to an Indian bank or insurer, and a Lyzr pitch outside HR.

  2. The companion deck.

    Product map, delivery plan, indicative pricing and a “verify before this goes out” list.

  3. Re-run one part.

    Swap a hypothesis or rewrite one slide without redoing the whole pitch.
